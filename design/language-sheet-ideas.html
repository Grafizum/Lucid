Lucid · language sheet

Three ways to open a list of languages.

The sheet was a heading and two hundred names. These are three answers to that — the first is what now ships in the app; the other two are the versions to reach for when the list wants more theatre. Every one of them floats clear of the bottom edge instead of docking to it, and every one is built from the same paper, clay and hairline as the rest of the app.

Idea 01 is in the build. Ideas 02 and 03 are studies.

01 monogram index

The list reads as a dictionary.

A language's first letter becomes a tile in front of its name, set in clay on the accent tint. The autonym sits above the name this phone calls it by, so the row is instantly recognisable in either direction — a person looking for 日本語 sees the character they know, not a translation of it.

  • Tile, not flag. A flag names a country; half these languages do not have one that would be honest.
  • Search is a control. A round well with the magnifier drawn inside it, sunk a shade into the sheet.
  • The choice said twice. Accent wash plus a clay medallion with a tick, so it never rests on colour alone.

Shipped
Raised 64 dp off the bottom edge, the sheet now floats like one more card on the page instead of a drawer pulled out of it.

02 alphabet rail

Two hundred names, one thumb.

The sheet opens on the app's own mark, then splits the question: what is already on this phone, and everything else. A letter rail down the right edge turns a long scroll into a gesture — hold a letter and the list jumps there.

  • The hero is the brand. Cream A and clay 文, drawn large, so the sheet is unmistakably Lucid's.
  • Segmented, not sectioned. "On this phone" first — the common move — with all 240 one tap away.
  • Rows without tiles. The names carry the weight, so the rail is the only ornament.

Cost
Needs a fast-scroll index and a second list state; roughly a day of work, and the rail is fiddly under a keyboard.

03 the world, softly

A map that shows which languages you already carry.

The header becomes a dotted field with a clay pin for every language whose words are downloaded. It is the most graphic of the three and the most legible at a glance: the picture answers "how much of the world does this phone speak" before a single word is read.

  • Pins, not borders. No country outlines — just points of light on a quiet field.
  • Recent as chips. The last three languages become tappable pills, above the search.
  • Quiet type. The map carries the drama so the list underneath can stay plain.

Cost
The pins need real coordinates for a few hundred languages, and the map must stay honest for right-to-left scripts. Best kept for a marketing page until that data exists.

What idea 01 actually changed

The sheet gained a marked header with the list's size, a pill search well with the magnifier inside it, monogram tiles on every row, the autonym over the phone's name, a wash and a clay tick on the row in force, and 64 dp of air under it. The same list is used by Settings, the tour and the lens, so all three moved together — and it still reads on paper by day and on charcoal at night, because every colour is a card role rather than a literal.
